Snowflake Competitive Battle Card 
By: Weyman Cohen

Last Updated: Dec 22th, 2025


1. Competitor History & Origin
* Snowflake was built for a world that never existed.
* Founded: 2012 - peak “big data” is coming era
* Assumption was everyone would query petabytes of data daily
* Reality: majority of queries fit on a single modern server, yet Snowflake forces them through complex, multi-node distributed systems 
* Why this matters: Snowflake’s architecture bakes in network overhead and cost from problems most teams will never face.
* MotherDuck POV: We’re built for how data teams actually work in the modern world. Serverless, unique node scaling model and dual execution. 
2. What to Know 
Architecture: 
* Hybrid architecture approach: Combines shared-disk (central data repository accessible to all compute nodes) with shared-nothing (MPP compute clusters that store portions of data locally), delivering both data management simplicity and performance scalability
* Three-layer structure: Database Storage (optimized columnar format with micro-partitions in cloud storage), Compute (independent virtual warehouses that don't share resources), and Cloud Services (coordinates security, metadata, query optimization, and infrastructure management)
* Fully managed cloud service: No hardware or software to install/configure, runs exclusively on public cloud infrastructure (AWS, Azure, GCP), with Snowflake handling all maintenance, upgrades, and tuning automatically


Pricing Structure
* Pay per warehouse, per second: All queries, loads, and DML require a running virtual warehouse. You’re billed in Snowflake credits per second (60-second minimum each start).
* Size = cost multiplier: Warehouse sizes scale from X-Small to 6X-Large, and each size step roughly doubles cost (e.g., X-Small ≈ 1 credit/hr → X-Large ≈ 16 → 5X-Large ≈ 256).
* Elastic but easy to overspend: Warehouses can be resized and multi-clustered on the fly—great for bursts, but costs rise linearly with size and concurrency, often leading to unpredictable bills at scale.


Bottom Line: For 99% of teams not querying petabytes daily, MotherDuck delivers much better price/performance with transparent pricing.


Strengths:
* Multi-petabyte data platforms with heavy governance, data marketplace, or cross-org data sharing requirements
* Deep ecosystem investment (Snowpark, Streamlit, extensive sharing) where migration costs outweigh benefits
* Hundreds of concurrent users requiring complex role hierarchies and fine-grained access controls
* 24/7 enterprise support SLAs and compliance certifications (SOC 2 Type II, HIPAA, etc.)


Weaknesses:
* Runaway costs by design: Accidental retries, bad defaults, and opaque pricing regularly create surprise bills (thousands to tens of thousands)
* Warehouse sprawl is inevitable: Hundreds of underutilized warehouses, exponential sizing jumps, broken resource monitors, and RBAC chaos create a permanent ops tax
* Price ≠ performance: Teams report faster queries and $30–40k/year savings after leaving Snowflake. High cost + latency hurts real-time use cases and unit economics


4. Why MotherDuck
1. Cost structure: We charge per query, not per warehouse-hour. For intermittent or ad-hoc analytics, this can be 70-90% cheaper. No paying for idle compute, plus Snowflake = minimum billable unit is a minute, whereas ours is a second
2. Hybrid execution: Queries run locally in DuckDB (even in-browser) and only use cloud compute when needed- saving time and money
3. No cold starts: DuckDB's embedded architecture means instant query execution. Snowflake warehouses need to spin up, causing delays and costs



5. Landmines 

* The Best Tool for the Job Mindset: Teams strategically diversify their data stack, using the right tool for each specific job rather than relying on a single solution for all use cases
* The Common Migration Pattern: Customers typically start with MotherDuck for customer-facing analytics and reporting alongside Snowflake, then expand to transformation layers as confidence builds—a pragmatic approach that begins with MotherDuck's strengths before broader adoption

* "We picked the tool that would solve our most complicated problem: transforming data slowly without sacrificing too much on the read side. The speed at which MotherDuck worked, the responsiveness, the support - there's been great chemistry between the two companies."


6. Objection Handling
* "Can MotherDuck scale to petabyte-level like Snowflake?"
   * Built for the 99% of workloads operating at terabyte scale (where most teams actually are). 
   * Delivers better performance at fraction of the cost for terabyte-scale (or lower) datasets. 
   * Can query 10s of terabytes at a time; 
      * DuckLake available for petabyte-level needs.
* "Snowflake has more integrations and ecosystem"
   * DuckDB/Motherduck: Rapid innovation velocity with consistent performance improvements. 
   * Partnership approach works closely with customers to prioritize missing features.
   * Early adopter advantage: partnering with innovative technology proves strategic over time.
* "Our team knows Snowflake - migration would be costly"
   * Speaks standard SQL, dbt works with minimal changes.
   * Most customers running in days; cost savings justify effort.
* "We're already committed to Snowflake spend"
   * Use MotherDuck alongside Snowflake for a hybrid approach that optimizes spend. Move serving-layer/gold-tier analytics, or other smaller and/or strategic workloads over to Motherduck at first. Keep Snowflake for certain workloads while reducing costs elsewhere.

8. Red Flags / When We Lose
Walk away if they:
* Actually query multi-petabyte data daily with heavy governance/compliance requirements
* Deeply invested in Snowflake ecosystem (Snowpark, Streamlit, extensive data sharing across orgs)
* Need 24/7 support SLAs and enterprise compliance certifications they can't compromise on

10. External Resources
1. As the CTO of FourSquare said, Snowflake built their business around a model that profit from compute consumption. Every time you write a suboptimal job, they make more money.
   1. This is part of the reason Snowflake decided to build a distributed (scale-out) architecture - they knew that using more nodes = more compute, and this inefficiency results in more revenue for them.
   2. MotherDuck, built on DuckDB’s single-node, serverless scale-up model, takes the opposite approach: we optimize for usage-efficiency and simplicity, not maximizing compute. We don't profit from inefficient compute, so our incentives are aligned with helping you do more with less.
